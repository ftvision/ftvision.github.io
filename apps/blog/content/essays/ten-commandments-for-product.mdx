# The Ten Commandments for Building Product

*A record of mistakes I made and watched others make — not a recipe for success. Following all ten earns you the right to maybe succeed; nothing earns you the success itself.*

There is one disease underneath most of what follows. Every shortcut that promises to spare you painful, first-hand contact with reality is an illusion that feels like progress and isn't. Reading logs instead of watching a user, awarding yourself the certainty of a great founder, letting an AI stand up the "correct" architecture before you've found a single customer — these feel like moving forward. They are ways of standing still while feeling busy. The product is an organism that has to evolve through contact with the world. It is not a spec you can get right in advance by thinking hard enough. These ten are defenses against the specific illusions that let you believe otherwise.

---

## 1. You shall be honest about whether you are committed to building a product or only in love with the idea; building means enduring its pain yourself, and any move to delegate that pain is proof the commitment was never there.

The pain I mean is not the engineering. You can delegate execution — plenty of people who could not write the code have shipped great products through people who could. The pain you cannot delegate is the grounding and the rejection: going to the user yourself, watching them ignore the thing you built, being told no to your face, and sitting in the uncertainty without a dashboard to hide behind. The moment you hand that off — "have someone run the user research and bring me the findings" — while keeping the decision authority for yourself, you have not delegated labor. You have delegated the only part that was yours, and you are now making product calls from an intuition you never earned, because you were never close enough to anyone to develop one.

I use one test. If I offered you a hundred million dollars to do pure research and never ship a product, would you take it? The person who is in love with the idea of being a product person says yes without flinching. The person who actually needs to build something says no, because the building is the point, not the title.

The most flattering disguise this wears is the belief that you are Steve Jobs or Elon Musk. You have their certainty and none of their scars. Read their interviews: they meant what they said, acted on it, and ate years of pain without quitting. The imitators borrow the conviction and skip the decade. It is completely fine to want to survive and to pivot. Just don't cosplay the commitment of people who didn't. This is 叶公好龙 — Lord Ye who loved dragons, covered his house in paintings of them, and fled in terror when a real one came to his window. Love the painted dragon if you like. Just don't claim you want the real one.

## 2. You shall not mistake self-expression for knowledge of your user; let your taste seed the product, but never let it govern.

Your taste is a legitimate seed. A point of view, a conviction, an aesthetic worth starting from — these are how good products begin. But a seed is a hypothesis, not knowledge. The moment you believe your taste always beats the evidence, you have stopped building a product and started building a monument to yourself.

The resolution that holds the line between this commandment and the next is simple: users own the problem; you own the solution. They are reliable witnesses to their own pain and unreliable architects of the cure. Mine them relentlessly for the problem. Never let them design the thing — and never let your self-expression pretend it has already understood them when all it has done is express you.

## 3. You shall not pretend to know your user; never let logs stand in for first-hand contact while your product is young.

There are two sins here, and the first is cruder than the second. The crude one is pretending you have nailed the customer. Not knowing who your user is is already bad, and the profile is genuinely hard to pin down — but faking it is worse, because the pretense stops you from going deeper and lets you build with confidence for the wrong group. The subtler sin is approximating the user through your logs.

So go sit with them and watch what they actually do. A real user interview is not transcribing what the user says — people's words are only part of it. It is watching how they use the thing, in their own environment, where the intricacies that one direct question would surface, and a dashboard never will, finally show themselves. This holds for business products too. The forward-deployed engineer exists precisely because someone has to sit inside the customer's reality rather than infer it from far away.

What I am rejecting is reaching for metrics *before you have formed a clear picture*. Logs are second-hand observation — digested, approximated, stripped of the context that made them mean anything — and they feel wonderful because they feel scalable. But scalable understanding is not the same thing as a successful product. Early on you are starving for *why*, and logs only ever tell you *what*. Worse, at the start you barely have the volume to mine anyway, so you are trading the one rich source you have for a thin one that flatters you with the illusion of scale. Get the raw, first-hand signal first. Earn the right to scale your understanding later.

## 4. You shall not mistake a breakthrough for a product; a capability is an input, not a finished good — the translation is the work.

This is not the tired tension between research and product. It is a specific arrogance: the belief that a research advance naturally becomes a good product on its own, that a good hammer entitles you to beat every nail and call the result a success. A capability is an input. A product is the translation of that input into a met need, and the translation — figuring out whose problem this solves, and how anyone would actually use it — is the entire job, not a footnote to the breakthrough.

The AI world is where I see this most nakedly. A researcher finds that a large model has some new capability and concludes, almost reflexively, that it should therefore be a product — without the faintest idea of whose problem it solves or how a person would hold it. The capability is real. The product is not there yet, and no amount of admiring the capability will summon it. Someone has to do the translation, and that someone is you.

## 5. You shall not throw the problem of how to use your product onto your users.

Great products carry clear functional affordance: the user simply *sees* how to use them. Good products carry a clear onboarding path that adapts the user to the product over time. Products built on genuinely new technology arrive with neither — and articulating the usage is your job, not the user's. Clear use is your design, not their puzzle to solve.

You pay for that articulation in one of two ways: by using the thing so heavily yourself that the right shape becomes obvious, or by working shoulder-to-shoulder with the people who will. New technology does not come with instructions. Throwing "figure out how to use this" at the user is taking your single hardest design problem and tossing it over the wall with their name on it.

## 6. You shall not treat every feature as equal, nor chase one more feature before launch; focus is the discipline of saying no.

Spreading your effort evenly across every feature, working hard on all of them, and feeling productive is how you avoid the only thing that matters. Focus is not working hard on everything. It is deciding what *not* to build, and that refusal is the muscle.

"One more feature before launch" is that muscle failing in real time. Each delay feels like care and is actually flight from the market's verdict. The good feeling you get from polishing the eleventh feature is the illusion thickening — the market has not been consulted, so you have learned nothing. Find the core set that gets the product into real hands, and ship that.

## 7. You shall be driven by outcomes, not activity, and ship early enough to face the verdict.

Shipping ten pull requests and five features is activity. It feels like progress and frequently isn't. The outcome is the only honest scoreboard, and the outcomes that count are things like retention and growth — whether people come back, whether the thing spreads — not how much you built this week.

So move your most uncomfortable moment, being rejected by real users, to the left. That moment is unavoidable; you only get to choose its timing, so take the pain early and make your later work easier. Then read the right number. Not signups, not applause, not vanity counts — *return*. Retention is never granted; you earn it. And when it is missing, resist the reflex to assume a single cause. It might be the wrong user, the wrong problem, a weak onboarding, a real but infrequent need, or an incumbent too entrenched to dislodge. Diagnose which one before you rebuild.

(This is the twin of commandment 6, and they are easy to confuse. Six is about *what you build* — the discipline of refusing scope. Seven is about *how you judge what you built, and when you expose it.* You can do one perfectly and fail the other.)

## 8. You shall not build for a million users before you have a thousand; your product is an organism, not a spec.

Standing up a system that holds a million users when you have zero feels like rigor. It is procrastination with excellent posture. If your product is going to take off, your alpha and beta will tell you in time to build the real infrastructure. Before you have a clear signal, building for a million is premature work on a future that hasn't arrived.

The sharper edge of this is new. Coding agents now make it *feel free* to build the final architecture on day one — as if you could skip exploration entirely and arrive directly at THE correct answer. That is the latest illusion, and AI is its salesman, because it sells the fantasy that you can be all-knowing at the start and never have to discover anything. You can't. The right system is found as the product finds its users. It grows like an organism. It is not dictated like a blueprint by someone who imagines he already sees the whole thing.

## 9. You shall not mistake a good product for a product people will pay for.

A good product and a product people will pay for are two different objects, governed by different forces, and conflating them has killed companies that genuinely built something good. Willingness to pay is shaped by economics you do not control — substitutes, supply and demand, customer behavior, what the alternative costs, and whose budget the money comes out of.

Payment does two jobs, and you need both. As validation, it is the only honest proof that the demand you think you found is real: stated enthusiasm is free and means almost nothing, while a credit card, a switch off an incumbent, or a signed contract means everything. As strategy, it forces the founder's real fork — build a high-quality product for a niche that genuinely pays, or invest in pricing and distribution to win a broader market. You have to decide which you are optimizing, because a high-quality product is not automatically a commercial one, and pretending the quality alone settles it is just another flavor of self-deception.

## 10. You shall not assume a good product earns attention for free.

Even the best product needs a path to the people it is for. The hard version of this — not the comfortable one — is that superior distribution routinely beats a superior product, and founders systematically under-invest in distribution because building feels noble and selling feels grubby. A conference launch is a luxury most of you will not get. The realistic task is figuring out how to survive in the open.

Timing belongs here too. Right product, wrong decade is one of the most common ways good work dies unseen. And all of it runs on attention, which is the scarce *input* you have to fight for — not the free *reward* a good product collects on its own. We are in an attention economy. Nobody is coming to find you.

---

*None of these will make your product succeed. They are how you avoid disqualifying yourself before you get the chance — table stakes, not a winning hand. People who did all ten still fail, and often. I am not handing you a formula. I am handing you the list of mistakes I already paid for, so that if you fail, you at least fail of something new.*
