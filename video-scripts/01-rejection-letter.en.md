### [0:20–1:40] THE EMAIL
🎙️ "Let me set the scene. Early 2024. I was at Citadel, quietly thinking about leaving. Out of nowhere, a founding engineer at Cursor — I'll call him Andy — finds my LinkedIn and cold-emails me. And it's a *good* email. Specific. Generous. The company's funded by OpenAI. The product's an AI-powered IDE already used by tens of thousands of programmers. And he's looking for a ninth person to join in San Francisco — someone who would, in his words, '*control a significant part of our product or ML research direction*,' built around whatever they cared about most. Would I want to interview?

Now — I got a lot of cold emails back then. I answered almost none of them. This one, I answered. And then I talked myself right out of it. My first reply deflected: cool project, but I don't think my stack fits your needs. There was even a typo that made it read like the *opposite* — like maybe I was a fit. Andy didn't take the hint. He wrote back — just elaborate, let's just chat, no interview required. So I made it official. I apologized for the 'miscommunication.' And the one thing I bothered to get right? Making my 'no' unmistakable. Not a full-stack engineer. No recent ML experience. I'll pass. Here's the actual thread."

🎬 The **redacted email screenshot** (`/images/cursor-email-2024.png`), slow push-in. Highlight: *"ninth"*, *"control a significant part"*, *"no interview required."*
📝 highlight phrases as they're spoken.

🎙️ "They held the door open. I shut it anyway — in writing — and left no room for doubt. I never asked them a single question. Not what they were building. Not what 'founding engineer' actually required. Not whether the gap I was so sure about even *existed*. I ran their screening for them. I reached the rejection on their behalf. And I mailed it to myself."
📝 `I mailed the rejection to myself.`

### [1:40–3:20] THE BAR I NEVER CHECKED
🎙️ "So here's what I had to sit with later. Look at what I disqualified myself on: not full-stack, no recent ML. Now look at what the role actually asked for. 'Superb technical ability.' 'Comfort with lots of autonomy.' And an area of interest they'd *build the job around*. The email basically said: we'll shape this around you. And I answered a requirement they had never made.

So where did my version of the bar even come from? Some of it was real — I was good at Python, I'd barely touched frontend. But the deeper problem: I was measuring myself against a standard I *invented*, and never tested. Part of it was Citadel and Google — years of deep work in in-house stacks, which, weirdly, narrowed my sense of what the rest of the industry was even doing. So I over-weighted everything I didn't know. And part of it was one startup call I'd taken not long before, where a cofounder wanted Rust and FastAPI — and I walked away feeling unqualified. Over **FastAPI**. Which is, what, a weekend of learning? I'd taken one stranger's checklist and quietly turned it into the standard I held myself to.

That's what self-rejection is. It wears the face of being realistic. It *feels* like humility — like you know your limits. But it's really pre-emptive surrender. You give up the option before anyone has actually evaluated you. The other side never gets to say yes — because you've already said no for them.

And look — not every no is a mistake. Sometimes it's judgment, not fear. But I'd read that email. I liked the project. I could picture myself using the product. That much interest earns you *one honest question* before you say no for them."

🎬 Split graphic: **"What I disqualified myself on"** (not full-stack · no ML) vs **"What the role actually asked"** (superb ability · autonomy · built around you). "FastAPI = a weekend" stamp. Recurring motif: "feels like humility" → crossed out → "pre-emptive surrender."
📝 `Self-rejection wears the face of being realistic.`

### [3:20–4:30] HOW IT ACTUALLY WENT (the honesty beat — keeps trust)
🎙️ "Now, I want to be honest with you, because this story does *not* have a clean 'if only' ending — and I'm not going to pretend it does. Cursor actually came back about a year later, while I was at a startup. I let the conversation sit. When I finally did interview — in 2026 — I failed the system design round. Partly because I wasn't prepared. Partly because, by then, I wasn't even sure I wanted it. The path and I were just never going to cross. And Andy — the guy who first emailed me — he'd moved on from Cursor within the year himself. Even the person on the 'winning' side of my story didn't ride it to the end. There was no alternate universe where I join, get rich, and live happily ever after. The outcome was never the point. The point is the conversation I *refused to have*."

🎬 Simple timeline: `2024 email` → `~2025 they come back` → `2026 interview — failed system design`. Footnote-style card: "Andy left Cursor within the year too."
📝 `The point isn't the job I lost. It's the conversation I refused to have.`

### [4:30–5:40] WHAT CHANGED
🎙️ "So since then, I've made myself exactly one rule. Take the call. Talk to the team. Let *them* be the ones to decide whether I fit. And what surprised me is how much was there, once I stopped gatekeeping myself. Some of these companies are genuinely fascinating. And the founders — a lot of them way younger than me — are humble, more mature in their thinking than their age suggests, and honestly inspiring to talk to. They're generous, too. One of them offered me **one-and-a-half million in equity** to come on as a founding engineer. Now — it's paper money. I know that. The number was never the point. The point is that these doors had been open the *whole time*. I was just the one who kept them shut."

🎬 Doors-opening motif; card: `$1.5M equity — paper money`; light montage of "interesting founders / SF" abstract.
📝 `These doors were open the whole time.`

### [5:40–6:10] PAYOFF + CTA
🎙️ "So this is the lesson I keep coming back to. The failures worth worrying about? They're not the interviews you fail. Those are honest no's — handed to you by someone who actually looked. The expensive ones are the no's you write *yourself*, in advance, and never send to anyone but you. So — **stop being your own rejection letter.**

If you've ever done this — talked yourself out of the room before you even walked in — tell me in the comments. I read them. And if this landed, subscribe; I write about building, careers, and AI, and I turn the essays into these. See you in the next one."
